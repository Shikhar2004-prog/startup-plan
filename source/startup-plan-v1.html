Startup plan: research-first AI edtech

Prepared for Shikhar Dubey and Sanhita Saxena · 10 November 2026 · Prices and numbers below are starting guesses to test, not facts.

One-line pitch. We take students who have never done research, put them through a mentored AI research project run by active IIT researchers, and help them turn that work into a paper, a portfolio and strong applications for research internships and AI jobs.

Your position: affordable, India-focused, mentored research by people who are doing research right now, ending in something real (a report, code, a paper draft) plus a strong application. No one in the list combines all of these for Indian college students.

What makes it different (and attractive)

  • Outcome over certificate. Every student leaves with a public GitHub repo, a 4 to 6 page report in conference format, and a short recorded talk.
  • Real research problems. Start from your own area: OCR, document verification, Indian-language documents. These are useful to industry too (banking KYC, govtech, legal).
  • "How research actually works" content. Reading papers, picking a question, baselines, ablations, writing, presenting. Almost nobody teaches this to undergrads.
  • Application coaching. A research CV, emails to professors, statements of purpose and interview practice for research internships.
  • Honest promise. Say "we prepare you to compete for research internships", never "we place you at an IIT". This builds trust.

Research Track outline

  • Weeks 1 to 2, foundations: Python and PyTorch refresher, how to read a paper, using Hugging Face, Git and Colab.
  • Weeks 3 to 7, project: pick a problem (for example OCR on Hindi forms, detecting tampered documents, table extraction), build a baseline, improve it, run ablations.
  • Weeks 8 to 9, write and present: Overleaf/LaTeX, figures, a workshop-style paper, a 5-minute talk.
  • Week 10, career: research CV, GitHub cleanup, cold emails to professors, internship applications, mock interviews.

4. Coding and tech strategy

Rule: don't build a platform first. Until about 100 paying students, every hour of coding should go into teaching material, not software.

Phase 0: launch with no code (months 0 to 6)

  • YouTube (unlisted for paid lessons), Google Meet or Zoom for live classes.
  • Notion as the course site, Google Forms for applications, Razorpay payment links for fees.
  • Discord for the community, GitHub Classroom or template repos for projects.
  • Optional: a hosted course platform (Graphy, TagMango or similar) if you want login and payments in one place.

Code you will write from day one (teaching code)

  • Starter repos and Colab notebooks for each project: data loading, baseline model, evaluation script.
  • Stack: Python, PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers, OpenCV. For OCR: Tesseract, PaddleOCR, TrOCR, Donut, LayoutLM-style models.
  • A shared evaluation script per project so student results can be compared fairly (like a small leaderboard).
  • Overleaf templates for reports and a slide template for talks.

Phase 1: your own site (6 to 18 months)

  • Next.js on Vercel, Supabase for login and database, Razorpay for payments.
  • A project tracker: each team's milestones, mentor comments and final report.
  • Public student showcase pages (your best marketing).

Phase 2: what makes it special at scale

  • AI assistant that gives first-pass feedback on student code and paper drafts, so mentors spend time on the hard parts.
  • Automatic leaderboard on shared benchmarks, and a "find a mentor" match with your PhD mentor network.

5. Video and YouTube content

Make one set of videos that serves both YouTube and the course. Free videos bring students in; deeper ones go in the paid track.

  • Free series: "How I got into research", "How to read an AI paper in 30 minutes", "Research internships in India: SRIP, Uplink and others explained", "OCR from scratch", "What professors look for in an intern's email".
  • Paid series: step-by-step project builds, writing a paper section by section, ablation studies, presenting your work.
  • Proof content: short clips of student demo days and before/after stories (with permission).
  • Rhythm: one long video and two Shorts per week is enough to start.

Key idea: the business grows by turning your best students into TAs and recruiting other PhD students as paid mentors. That is how a two-person team becomes a hundred-mentor network without losing quality.

8. Risks and rules to check

  • PhD rules. Many institutes have rules on outside work, stipends and startups for PhD scholars. Check yours and talk to your advisor and incubator before charging money.
  • IIT name and logo. Don't use them in branding. You can state facts, like "founded by IIT researchers".
  • No placement guarantees. Promise preparation and support, never seats.
  • Research ethics. Students must do their own work. Use only public or properly licensed datasets, especially for identity documents.
  • Time. Two founders with PhDs can burn out. Cap cohort size until TAs exist.

9. A short note on balancing

Give each thing a fixed slot instead of fitting it in when you can. For example, GATE and exam prep in the morning, startup work in the evening, and one fixed weekend block for recording. Your YouTube channel and the startup should be the same effort: every video you record for the course is also YouTube content. Review the week every Sunday and cut whatever isn't moving students forward.
